CANCER GUIDE · 01

Lung Cancer

Understand lung anatomy, cancer types, symptoms, diagnostic tests, procedures, staging and treatment options.

For patients and families: a guide to help you prepare for conversations with your healthcare team.

Educational information only. This page cannot diagnose cancer or choose treatment for an individual.

1. Understanding lung cancer

Lung cancer begins when abnormal cells in the lung grow out of control. It can start in different lung structures and may spread to nearby lymph nodes or other parts of the body.

Important: Symptoms and risk factors cannot confirm or rule out cancer. Diagnosis usually requires examination of cells or tissue when clinically appropriate.

An example location is highlighted. Real tumours can occur in different places and appear differently on scans.

2. Main types of lung cancer

Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC)

The most common broad group. Includes adenocarcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma and large cell carcinoma, among other less common types.

Small cell lung cancer (SCLC)

A distinct type that often grows and spreads more quickly. Treatment approaches differ from NSCLC.

Subtype is determined by examining tumour cells or tissue. It matters because treatment options differ by type and stage. NCI: Lung Cancer.

3. Risk factors and prevention

Smoking is a major risk factor, but people who never smoked can also develop lung cancer. Other associated factors include second-hand smoke, radon, certain workplace exposures, air pollution, prior chest radiation and personal or family history. Their importance varies.

Risk-reduction steps

  • Avoid tobacco and seek support to quit.
  • Reduce second-hand smoke exposure.
  • Follow workplace safety guidance for hazardous substances.
  • Discuss personal risk and screening with a clinician.

Myth vs fact

Myth: Only people who smoke get lung cancer.

Fact: People who never smoked can also develop it.

4. Possible symptoms

Early lung cancer may cause no symptoms. These signs can also have other causes, but persistent or worsening symptoms should be medically assessed.

Chest and breathing

  • Cough that does not go away or worsens
  • Coughing up blood
  • Shortness of breath or wheezing
  • Chest pain or discomfort
  • Repeated chest infections

Other changes

  • Hoarse voice or difficulty swallowing
  • Unexplained weight loss or appetite loss
  • Unusual fatigue
  • Swelling in face or neck
Seek urgent medical care for severe breathing difficulty, heavy coughing of blood, blue/grey lips, fainting or severe chest pain.

5. Screening

Screening tests people without symptoms. Low-dose CT (LDCT) is used for selected people at increased risk in programmes with defined eligibility criteria. It is different from a diagnostic scan for someone with symptoms.

Eligibility varies by country and guideline and may depend on age and smoking history. Ask a clinician whether screening is appropriate. An abnormal screening result does not automatically mean cancer.

Read NCI lung cancer resources and NCI CT scan information.

6. Tests used to investigate lung cancer

Not everyone needs every test. The team chooses based on symptoms, examination, scan findings and what information is needed.

PET-CTMetabolic and anatomical information to help assess possible spread.Some non-cancer conditions can also show uptake.

The oncology team selects tests according to symptoms, scan findings, suspected or confirmed cancer type, stage, treatment plan, medical history and local availability. Some tests are alternatives to one another.

Assessment / testWhy it may be usedImportant note
Medical history and physical examinationReviews symptoms, smoking and exposure history, previous illnesses, medicines, family history, weight and general condition.Part of assessment; it cannot confirm cancer by itself.
Oxygen saturation (pulse oximetry)Checks oxygen level at the fingertip.May be done at rest, during activity or during follow-up.

Test / procedureSample or purposeKey point
Flexible bronchoscopy with biopsy / brushings / washingsSamples a lesion or cells from the airways.Useful for selected central or airway-accessible abnormalities.
EBUS-TBNA (endobronchial ultrasound-guided needle aspiration)Samples selected lymph nodes or lesions near the airways.Can provide diagnosis and staging information.
Radial EBUS, navigational or robotic bronchoscopyHelps reach selected peripheral lung lesions for sampling.Availability and suitability vary by centre and lesion.
CT-guided transthoracic needle biopsyCollects tissue from a suitable lesion through the chest wall.Risks can include pneumothorax and bleeding; clinician explains individual risk.
Fine-needle aspiration (FNA) or core needle biopsyCollects cells or a tissue core from a lung lesion, lymph node or another suspicious site.Core tissue may provide more material, but the best method depends on the target.
EUS-FNA / EUS-guided samplingSamples selected structures or lymph nodes near the oesophagus using endoscopic ultrasound.Used in selected staging situations.
Mediastinoscopy or surgical lymph-node samplingSamples lymph nodes in the central chest.May be considered if less invasive staging tests are inconclusive and the result matters.
Thoracoscopy / VATS biopsySamples pleura, lung or other chest structures under direct visualisation.Used when clinically appropriate, including when other sampling is insufficient.
Thoracentesis with pleural-fluid testsRemoves fluid around the lung for cytology and, where appropriate, additional laboratory tests.A negative fluid result does not always exclude cancer; further evaluation may be needed.
Sputum cytologyExamines coughed-up mucus for cancer cells.Has limited sensitivity and is used only in selected circumstances; a negative result cannot rule out cancer.

Surgical lung biopsy or resection specimenProvides tissue when surgery is appropriate or other samples are not adequate.Not the first choice for everyone; decisions depend on risk and treatment intent.

6D. Laboratory tests on biopsy, cells and pleural fluid

Laboratory testWhat it helps determineWhen / limitation
Histopathology (tissue examination)Whether cancer is present and its tissue pattern.Usually central to confirming the diagnosis when a tissue sample is obtained.
Cytology (cells in fluid or brushings)Whether sampled cells look malignant.Used for suitable fluid or cell samples; may not provide enough material for every further test.
Immunohistochemistry (IHC)Uses protein markers to help classify the tumour and distinguish lung primary cancer from other tumour types.Examples may include TTF-1, Napsin A, p40 and, for suspected neuroendocrine tumours, synaptophysin, chromogranin or INSM1. The panel is selected by the pathologist.
PD-L1 immunohistochemistryMeasures PD-L1 expression in tumour tissue to help inform certain immunotherapy decisions.Relevant in many NSCLC treatment pathways; interpretation depends on tumour type and clinical context.
Molecular profiling / next-generation sequencing (NGS)Looks for actionable gene changes or fusions that may affect targeted treatment options.Testing strategy depends on subtype, stage, tissue adequacy and local guidance.
Single-gene molecular tests (PCR or other methods)May test a specific mutation or alteration when appropriate.May complement or be used when a broad panel is unavailable or unsuitable.
FISH or other fusion/rearrangement testingCan identify selected gene rearrangements or copy-number changes.Used when needed based on pathology and molecular results.
RNA-based fusion testingCan help detect selected gene fusions.May be part of a comprehensive molecular panel.
Liquid biopsy / circulating tumour DNA (ctDNA) from bloodLooks for tumour-derived genetic changes in the bloodstream.May help when tissue is insufficient or difficult to obtain. A negative result may miss alterations and does not always replace tissue testing.
Microbiology of sputum or pleural fluid (such as bacterial culture, and selected TB/fungal tests)Investigates infection or another condition that may resemble or coexist with cancer.Only when clinically indicated; these tests do not diagnose lung cancer itself.
Review of pathology slides / second pathology opinionCan clarify an uncertain or complex diagnosis or subtype.Considered when results are unclear or a specialist review may change care.

Examples of molecular biomarkers sometimes tested in NSCLC: EGFR, ALK, ROS1, BRAF V600E, KRAS (including G12C), MET exon 14 skipping, RET, NTRK fusions and ERBB2/HER2 alterations. Other biomarkers or a broader panel may be appropriate under current local guidance. Not every tumour needs every individual assay, and the exact panel should be determined by the oncology/pathology team.

6G. Tests used during and after treatment

  • Repeat CBC, kidney/liver chemistry and electrolytes: frequency depends on chemotherapy, targeted therapy, immunotherapy, symptoms and local protocol.
  • Repeat thyroid, glucose, cortisol or other hormone tests: when required by the medicine being used or symptoms, especially for immune-related side effects.
  • Follow-up CT or other imaging: assesses response, complications or possible recurrence according to the treatment plan.
  • Repeat biopsy or molecular testing: may be considered if the cancer changes or progresses and the result could alter treatment.
  • Oxygen assessment, ECG or other organ tests: when symptoms, comorbidities or treatment risks warrant them.

6H. Tests that are not routine stand-alone lung-cancer screening or confirmation tests

Blood tumour markers such as CEA, CYFRA 21-1, NSE and ProGRP may be used in selected specialist contexts, but they are not reliable stand-alone tests to screen for or confirm lung cancer. Routine blood counts, ESR/CRP, sputum tests, or a normal X-ray also cannot independently rule cancer in or out. A clinician should explain what decision a proposed test is expected to inform.

How to use this checklist at an appointment: Ask which tests are needed now, which are optional or alternatives, which results are still pending, whether the sample is sufficient for subtype and biomarker testing, how results will change the plan, and when results are expected. Do not delay urgent assessment while trying to complete a checklist yourself.

Clinical reference links: American Cancer Society — How lung cancer is diagnosed; NICE — Lung cancer diagnosis and staging; NCI — Tests and procedures used to diagnose cancer; NCI — Biomarker testing.

7. Procedures and how samples are collected

The safest and most useful sampling method depends on where the abnormal area is and the person's health.

This is an educational concept illustration, not instructions for performing a procedure.

Bronchoscopy

A thin flexible camera tube is passed through the nose or mouth into the airways. The clinician may inspect and collect samples.

Image-guided needle biopsy

A needle may be guided by CT or other imaging to collect a sample from a suitable abnormal area.

EBUS / lymph-node sampling

Endobronchial ultrasound helps clinicians view structures near the airways and sample selected lymph nodes.

Thoracentesis

If fluid has collected around the lung, a clinician may remove fluid for testing and sometimes to ease symptoms.

Thoracoscopy

A camera and instruments enter through small chest openings to examine the area and, when needed, collect tissue.

Pathology

A pathologist examines cells or tissue to determine whether cancer is present and help identify its type.

Ask before a procedure: Why is it needed? How should I prepare? What risks apply to me? When will results arrive? Who should I contact afterward?

8. Understanding staging

Staging describes the extent of cancer and helps plan care. For NSCLC, doctors commonly use TNM: tumour (T), nearby lymph nodes (N), and metastasis/spread to distant sites (M). SCLC is also often described as limited-stage or extensive-stage.

T — Tumour

Size and local growth of the primary tumour.

N — Nodes

Whether nearby lymph nodes contain cancer.

M — Metastasis

Whether cancer has spread to distant sites.

Only the clinical team can assign the stage using pathology, imaging and other findings.

9. Treatment options

Treatment depends on type, stage, tumour location, biomarkers, lung function, general health and patient preferences. Options may be used alone or in combination.

Surgery

May remove a tumour and some surrounding tissue in selected cases, especially for localised disease when surgery is appropriate.

Radiotherapy

Uses carefully planned radiation to treat a target area, alone or with other treatments.

Chemotherapy

Medicines kill or slow cancer-cell growth. Regimens depend on type and clinical circumstances.

Immunotherapy

Helps the immune system recognise or attack cancer in certain settings.

Targeted therapy

Targets particular molecular changes in some cancers; tumour testing may identify options.

Combined treatment / clinical trials

Some care plans combine therapies. A trial may be an option for some people after discussing benefits and risks.

There is no single treatment for all lung cancers. Do not start, stop or change medicines based on this general guide. Discuss the plan with a qualified oncology team.

10. Biomarker testing

For some lung cancers, particularly many NSCLCs, testing tumour tissue for gene changes or proteins can help doctors consider targeted therapy or immunotherapy. The appropriate tests depend on the diagnosis and clinical setting.

A result does not guarantee that a medicine will work. Ask whether there is enough tissue, which tests are recommended, how long results take and whether the results may change treatment choices.

NCI: Biomarker Testing for Cancer Treatment.

11. Side effects, supportive care and follow-up

Side effects vary by treatment and person. Ask which symptoms are expected, which need same-day advice and which are emergencies. Supportive care can help with pain, breathlessness, nutrition, fatigue, emotional distress and practical needs.

  • Keep a list of medicines, allergies and symptoms.
  • Ask for written instructions and an after-hours contact number.
  • Ask before taking supplements or herbal products; some interact with treatment.
  • Attend follow-up visits and ask what tests are planned.
For severe breathing difficulty, heavy bleeding, sudden confusion or severe chest pain, seek emergency care.

12. Questions to ask the doctor

  1. What exact type and subtype is confirmed?
  2. Which tests confirm the diagnosis and which are pending?
  3. What is the stage, and how was it determined?
  4. Is biomarker testing recommended, and could it affect treatment?
  5. What options fit this specific case, and what is the goal of each?
  6. What benefits, side effects and alternatives should we understand?
  7. Should we consider a second opinion or clinical trial?
  8. Who should we contact if symptoms worsen or side effects appear?
