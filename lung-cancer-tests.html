Cancer-specific investigation guide

Lung Cancer
Tests & Procedures

A comprehensive educational reference covering investigations that may be used when lung cancer is suspected, diagnosed, staged or followed over time.

The appropriate tests depend on the clinical situation, suspected cancer type, location of an abnormality, stage, overall health and the information the medical team needs.

Important: This page explains tests and procedures only. It does not diagnose lung cancer and does not tell an individual patient which tests they personally need. Investigation plans must be determined and interpreted by qualified healthcare professionals.

1. Clinical Assessment

Evaluation normally begins by understanding symptoms, medical history and examination findings. These findings help determine which investigations may be appropriate.

Clinical

Medical history

Symptoms, duration, smoking history, occupational exposures, previous illnesses, previous cancers and relevant family history may be reviewed.

Clinical

Respiratory assessment

Breathing symptoms, cough, sputum, coughing blood, chest discomfort and other respiratory findings may be assessed.

Examination

Physical examination

The clinician may examine the chest, lymph nodes, abdomen, skin and other systems depending on symptoms and findings.

Functional

Performance / functional assessment

General functional status and ability to perform normal activities may be documented.

Measurements

Vital signs

Blood pressure, pulse, respiratory rate, oxygen saturation and temperature may be measured.

Risk assessment

Exposure assessment

Smoking, second-hand smoke, occupational exposures, environmental exposures and relevant previous treatments may be reviewed.

2. Screening

Lung-cancer screening is different from investigating symptoms. Screening is intended for selected people at sufficiently increased risk according to applicable guidelines and eligibility criteria.

Low-dose CT (LDCT)

Low-dose CT is the principal imaging test used in established lung-cancer screening programmes for eligible higher-risk populations.

Screening eligibility assessment

Age, smoking exposure and other risk factors may be assessed according to the screening programme being used.

High-risk surveillance

People with particular inherited or clinical risk factors may have individualized surveillance plans.

Follow-up of screening findings

An abnormal screening result may lead to diagnostic imaging or other investigations. A screening abnormality is not automatically a cancer diagnosis.

Screening is not diagnosis. A diagnostic evaluation may be required when symptoms or an abnormal screening result are present.

3. Blood Tests

Routine blood tests generally do not establish a diagnosis of lung cancer by themselves. They can provide information about general health, organ function, complications and readiness for procedures or treatment.

Complete blood count (CBC/FBC)

Measures red cells, white cells and platelets.

White-cell differential

Provides information about different white-cell populations.

Peripheral blood smear

Microscopic examination of blood cells when clinically indicated.

Kidney function

Creatinine, eGFR, urea and related measurements may be assessed.

Liver function

Selected liver enzymes, bilirubin, albumin and other measurements may be assessed.

Electrolytes

Sodium, potassium and other electrolytes may be measured.

Calcium

Calcium may be checked, particularly when clinically relevant to symptoms or disease extent.

Magnesium & phosphate

May be measured when clinically indicated.

Glucose / HbA1c

May be checked as part of general medical assessment.

LDH

May provide additional clinical information but is not a stand-alone diagnostic test for lung cancer.

CRP / ESR

Markers of inflammation that may be used when clinically relevant.

Coagulation tests

PT/INR, aPTT and related tests may be required before selected procedures or when bleeding risk is relevant.

Blood group & crossmatch

May be required when an invasive procedure or surgery could involve significant blood loss.

Infection-related tests

Hepatitis B/C, HIV or other infection testing may be performed when clinically or procedurally indicated.

Pregnancy testing

May be relevant before selected imaging, procedures or treatments in people who could be pregnant.

4. Urine Tests

Urine investigations are not usually the primary tests used to diagnose a lung tumour, but may be performed for general health assessment, symptoms, infection or preparation for procedures.

Urinalysis

General urine assessment when clinically indicated.

Urine microscopy

Microscopic examination for cells and other findings.

Urine culture

May be used when urinary infection is suspected.

Specialized urine testing

Additional assays may be ordered for specific medical questions unrelated or indirectly related to the lung tumour.

5. Other Body-Fluid Tests

If lung cancer is associated with fluid around the lung or elsewhere, fluid may be sampled and examined.

Pleural-fluid analysis

Fluid from around the lung may be analyzed for cells, chemistry and other findings.

Pleural-fluid cytology

Cells in pleural fluid may be examined for malignant cells.

Thoracentesis

A procedure used to remove pleural fluid for diagnostic testing or other clinical purposes.

Pericardial-fluid testing

If fluid surrounds the heart, selected cases may require analysis of pericardial fluid.

CSF testing

Cerebrospinal-fluid examination may be considered in selected situations involving the nervous system.

Microbiology of fluid samples

When infection is part of the differential diagnosis, fluid may undergo microbiological testing.

6. Imaging

Imaging can help identify suspicious lung abnormalities, characterize lesions, assess lymph nodes, look for spread and monitor changes over time.

Chest X-ray

Provides a broad view of the chest and may reveal an abnormal area requiring further investigation.

Diagnostic chest CT

Provides detailed cross-sectional images of the lungs, mediastinum, lymph nodes and nearby structures.

Contrast-enhanced CT

Contrast may improve assessment of selected structures and clinical questions.

Thin-section CT

High-resolution or thin-section imaging can provide detailed assessment of pulmonary abnormalities.

CT chest-abdomen-pelvis

May be used to evaluate the primary tumour and possible disease outside the chest.

CT-guided biopsy

CT imaging may guide a needle toward a suitable peripheral lung lesion or other target.

PET-CT

Combines metabolic information with CT anatomy and can help assess the extent of disease.

PET-MRI

Combines PET information with MRI and may be used in selected clinical settings.

Brain MRI

May be used to assess the brain for possible metastatic disease when clinically indicated.

Brain CT

May be used when MRI is unavailable, unsuitable or when urgent assessment is needed.

Bone scan

Nuclear medicine imaging that may be used to evaluate suspected bone involvement in selected circumstances.

SPECT / SPECT-CT

Specialized nuclear imaging may be used for selected diagnostic questions.

Ultrasound

May assess accessible lymph nodes, pleural fluid, liver lesions or other structures.

Doppler ultrasound

May assess blood vessels when vascular questions arise.

Thoracic ultrasound

Can help assess pleural fluid and guide selected procedures.

Fluoroscopy

Real-time X-ray imaging can be used in selected procedures including image-guided sampling.

7. Endoscopy

Endoscopic procedures can directly inspect the airways or nearby structures and may allow tissue, cell or fluid sampling.

Flexible bronchoscopy

A camera-equipped scope is passed into the airways to inspect abnormal areas and obtain samples.

Rigid bronchoscopy

A larger rigid scope may be used for selected diagnostic or interventional airway procedures.

Bronchial biopsy

Tissue may be collected from an abnormal area inside the airways.

Bronchial brushing

A brush may collect cells from the airway surface for cytological examination.

Bronchial washing

Fluid introduced into the airways may be collected for cellular or microbiological analysis.

Bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL)

A lavage sample may be collected during bronchoscopy for selected diagnostic questions.

Endobronchial ultrasound (EBUS)

Ultrasound attached to a bronchoscope can visualize structures and lymph nodes around the airways.

EBUS-TBNA

A needle can be passed through the airway wall to sample lymph nodes or other targets.

Endoscopic oesophageal ultrasound (EUS)

An ultrasound endoscope through the oesophagus can visualize nearby lymph nodes and permit needle sampling in selected cases.

Navigation bronchoscopy

Computer-assisted or electromagnetic navigation may help reach selected peripheral lung lesions.

Robotic-assisted bronchoscopy

Robotic navigation may be used in selected centres for sampling peripheral lung lesions.

8. Biopsy & Sampling Procedures

A tissue or cell sample is often required to establish the diagnosis and classify the tumour. The safest and most useful sampling method depends on where the abnormality is located.

Fine-needle aspiration (FNA)

A thin needle obtains cells or small fragments of tissue.

Core needle biopsy

A larger needle obtains cylinders of tissue that can provide more material for pathology and biomarker testing.

Transthoracic needle biopsy

A needle is passed through the chest wall into a suitable lung lesion, often with CT guidance.

CT-guided lung biopsy

CT imaging helps guide the biopsy needle toward a selected lesion.

Transbronchial biopsy

Tissue is obtained through a bronchoscope.

Transbronchial needle aspiration

Needle sampling through the bronchial wall can obtain cells from selected lymph nodes or lesions.

EBUS-guided lymph-node sampling

EBUS can guide sampling of mediastinal and hilar lymph nodes.

EUS-guided needle sampling

Selected lymph nodes or nearby structures can be sampled through the oesophagus.

Mediastinoscopy

A surgical procedure can directly examine and sample selected mediastinal lymph nodes.

Mediastinotomy

An alternative surgical approach may be used to access selected mediastinal structures when necessary.

Thoracoscopy

Can inspect the pleural space and obtain tissue or fluid samples.

VATS sampling

Video-assisted thoracic surgery may provide access to selected lung, pleural or lymph-node targets.

Metastatic-site biopsy

If disease has spread, a safer or more informative accessible metastatic site may sometimes be sampled.

Pleural biopsy

Selected pleural abnormalities can be sampled when pleural involvement is suspected.

Surgical specimen

If a lung lesion is removed surgically, the specimen undergoes detailed pathological examination.

Sputum cytology

Coughed-up sputum can be examined for abnormal cells. It is not sufficient for every suspected lung cancer.

9. Pathology

Pathology examines tissue, cells or other samples to establish and classify the diagnosis. The pathology report may distinguish primary lung cancer from cancer that has spread to the lung from another organ.

Histopathology

Microscopic examination of tissue architecture and tumour cells.

Cytopathology

Microscopic examination of individual cells or small cell groups from fluid or needle samples.

Histologic classification

Determines the type and subtype of lung cancer where possible.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Uses antibodies to detect proteins that help classify the tumour and distinguish possible origins.

Special stains

Selected stains may provide additional information about tumour characteristics or differential diagnoses.

PD-L1 immunohistochemistry

Measures PD-L1 expression using an appropriate validated assay when relevant to clinical decision-making.

Ki-67 / proliferation assessment

May be used in selected tumour types or differential diagnoses.

Flow cytometry

May be useful when a lymphoid or hematologic malignancy is part of the differential diagnosis.

Pathology review

Expert review of existing slides or tissue may be requested when diagnosis or classification is uncertain.

Margin assessment

Surgical specimens may be examined to determine whether tumour extends to a resection margin.

Lymph-node assessment

Sampled or removed lymph nodes can be examined for tumour involvement.

10. Molecular & Genomic Testing

Molecular testing is particularly important in many non-small cell lung cancers. Testing may examine tumour tissue, blood or another appropriate specimen.

Single-gene testing

A specific gene alteration may be tested when appropriate.

Multigene panel

Multiple genes can be assessed simultaneously to identify clinically relevant alterations.

Next-generation sequencing (NGS)

NGS can evaluate multiple genomic alterations from a tumour specimen or, in selected circumstances, blood.

DNA sequencing

Can identify selected mutations and other genomic changes.

RNA sequencing

Can help identify RNA-level alterations including some gene fusions.

Fusion testing

May detect clinically relevant gene rearrangements or fusions.

FISH

Fluorescence in situ hybridization can detect selected gene rearrangements or copy-number abnormalities.

PCR-based testing

PCR can be used for specific mutations, fusions or other molecular targets.

Copy-number analysis

Can assess selected gene amplifications or deletions.

Microsatellite instability (MSI)

May be assessed in selected tumours where clinically relevant.

Mismatch-repair (MMR)

MMR proteins may be evaluated using appropriate molecular or IHC methods in selected settings.

Tumour mutational burden (TMB)

A genomic measure that may be available through comprehensive molecular profiling in selected settings.

Liquid biopsy

Blood can be analyzed for tumour-derived DNA or other tumour-related material.

Circulating tumour DNA (ctDNA)

Fragments of tumour-derived DNA in blood may be analyzed for selected genomic alterations.

PD-L1 testing

PD-L1 protein expression may be assessed by IHC in appropriate lung cancer settings.

Important: The exact molecular panel should not be assumed from this list. Testing depends on the lung-cancer subtype, stage, specimen quality, laboratory methods and applicable clinical guidelines.

11. Inherited / Germline Genetic Testing

Inherited genetic testing is different from testing the tumour itself. It may be considered when personal or family history suggests a hereditary cancer syndrome or when a specialist considers germline testing appropriate.

Genetic counselling

Assessment of personal and family history before or alongside germline testing.

Germline testing

Examines inherited DNA, usually from blood or another validated sample.

Hereditary cancer panel

Multiple inherited cancer-associated genes may be tested together when appropriate.

Family / cascade testing

Testing of relatives may be considered when a clinically important inherited variant has been identified.

12. Blood-Cancer Investigations

A lung or mediastinal mass can sometimes represent lymphoma, leukemia-related disease or another hematologic malignancy rather than a primary lung carcinoma. Additional blood or tissue investigations may therefore be needed in selected cases.

Peripheral blood smear

Microscopic evaluation of blood cells.

Bone-marrow aspiration

May be performed when a hematologic malignancy or marrow involvement is suspected.

Bone-marrow biopsy

Provides tissue architecture for selected hematologic investigations.

Flow cytometry

Can characterize abnormal blood or tissue cell populations.

Immunophenotyping

Uses cell-surface or intracellular markers to help classify abnormal cells.

Cytogenetics

Chromosome analysis may be performed in selected hematologic conditions.

FISH

Can detect selected chromosomal or gene abnormalities.

Molecular hematology testing

Selected molecular tests may be performed if a blood malignancy is suspected.

Minimal residual disease testing

MRD testing may be relevant when a hematologic malignancy has been diagnosed.

13. Staging Investigations

After lung cancer is diagnosed, additional investigations may determine how extensive the disease is. For NSCLC, staging commonly uses the TNM system. SCLC may also be described using limited-stage and extensive-stage groupings.

Staging question Possible investigations Purpose
Primary tumour Chest CT, contrast CT, MRI in selected circumstances Assess tumour size, location and local relationships.
Regional lymph nodes CT, PET-CT, EBUS-TBNA, EUS-FNA, mediastinoscopy Assess whether regional nodes contain cancer.
Brain Brain MRI or CT when appropriate Assess possible intracranial spread.
Bones PET-CT, bone scan or targeted imaging Assess suspected bone involvement.
Liver/adrenal/abdominal organs CT, MRI, PET-CT or ultrasound when appropriate Assess possible distant disease.
Pleura CT, PET-CT, thoracentesis, pleural cytology or biopsy Assess possible pleural involvement.
Distant lesions Targeted imaging and/or biopsy Confirm or characterize suspected metastatic disease when needed.

14. Pre-Treatment / Pre-Procedure Investigations

Some investigations are performed to establish baseline health, assess procedural risk or determine whether an intervention can be safely performed.

Complete blood count

Baseline blood-cell measurements.

Kidney function

Important when assessing overall health and selected procedures or contrast-related considerations.

Liver function

Baseline organ-function assessment.

Coagulation profile

May be needed before selected invasive procedures.

ECG

May assess cardiac rhythm and baseline cardiac status.

Echocardiogram

May be performed when cardiac function needs assessment.

Pulmonary-function tests

May be particularly important when lung surgery is being considered.

Spirometry

Measures airflow and is a component of pulmonary-function assessment.

DLCO

Measures the lung's ability to transfer gas and may contribute to assessment before lung surgery.

Arterial blood gas

Measures oxygen, carbon dioxide and acid-base status in selected circumstances.

Anaesthesia assessment

Pre-procedure evaluation may assess airway, cardiac, respiratory and other risks.

Pregnancy testing

May be required where pregnancy is possible and relevant to the planned investigation.

15. Treatment-Response Monitoring Tests

Although this page does not cover treatment itself, tests may be used to determine whether disease is changing after treatment has begun.

Repeat CT

Can compare tumour and lymph-node findings with previous imaging.

Repeat PET-CT

May be used in selected circumstances to assess metabolic changes.

Repeat MRI

May monitor previously identified brain or other lesions.

Repeat blood tests

Can monitor blood counts, kidney function, liver function and other relevant parameters.

Repeat molecular testing

May be considered when disease changes and new molecular information is clinically useful.

ctDNA testing

Blood-based molecular testing may be used in selected clinical settings.

Biopsy of a changing lesion

A new or changing lesion may sometimes require tissue confirmation.

16. Recurrence & Follow-Up Investigations

After initial treatment, surveillance plans vary according to cancer type, stage, treatment history and clinical circumstances.

Clinical follow-up

History, symptoms and physical examination may be reviewed periodically.

Surveillance CT

CT may be used at intervals determined by the clinical team.

Chest imaging

Additional chest imaging may be used when clinically appropriate.

PET-CT

May be used for selected questions rather than as a universal routine surveillance test.

Brain imaging

May be repeated when symptoms or prior disease make brain assessment clinically relevant.

Repeat biopsy

A suspicious new or recurrent lesion may require tissue sampling.

Repeat molecular profiling

May be considered when a new tissue sample is obtained and molecular information could affect clinical decisions.

Laboratory monitoring

Blood tests may be performed based on symptoms, treatment history and overall health.

17. Specialist & Advanced Investigations / Procedures

Some investigations are performed only in selected patients or specialist centres.

Electromagnetic navigation bronchoscopy

Computer-assisted navigation may help reach selected peripheral pulmonary lesions.

Robotic-assisted bronchoscopy

Robotic platforms may assist selected peripheral lesion sampling.

Advanced EBUS

Specialist EBUS techniques may be used for detailed mediastinal or hilar assessment.

Rapid onsite cytologic evaluation (ROSE)

In selected centres, a preliminary specimen assessment can help determine whether material appears adequate for the planned analysis.

Thoracoscopy / VATS

Can provide access to selected pleural, lung and lymph-node targets.

Mediastinoscopy

Surgical access to selected mediastinal lymph nodes when less invasive sampling is insufficient or unsuitable.

Expert pathology review

Specialist review may be requested when the diagnosis or tumour classification is uncertain.

Comprehensive genomic profiling

Broad molecular profiling may be used in appropriate lung-cancer settings.

Liquid-biopsy profiling

Blood-based tumour DNA analysis may provide molecular information when tissue is unavailable or insufficient in selected situations.

Important Interpretation Points

No single test diagnoses every lung cancer. A diagnosis may require a combination of clinical assessment, imaging, tissue or cell sampling and laboratory/pathology examination.
Biopsy and pathology matter. Imaging can strongly suggest a lung tumour, but tissue or cell examination is often required to establish and classify the diagnosis.
Not every patient needs every investigation. The investigation pathway depends on symptoms, imaging findings, tumour location, suspected subtype, stage and the patient's overall health.
Molecular testing is not one single test. Different molecular methods and panels detect different alterations, and the appropriate testing strategy depends on the clinical setting and available validated assays.
Tests listed as “specialist”, “selected” or “advanced” are not routine requirements for everyone. Their inclusion means they are recognized tools that may be used in appropriate circumstances.
